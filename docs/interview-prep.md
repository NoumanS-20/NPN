# Interview preparation

For the technical interview on **30 September 2026**.

**How to use this.** Section 1 is the numbers — learn those. Section 2 explains
each part of the system in plain words: what it does, why we built it that way,
what we would change. Section 3 is the questions we expect, with answers. Section
4 is the four things we say before anyone has to ask.

**Every number here is real.** It comes from `data/processed/*/metrics.json` or
from a live API call. Nothing is rounded up to sound better.

**All money is in rupees.** The procurement dataset is American and its money is
in dollars, so we convert once, when the data is read, at **₹83 = $1**. That rate
lives in one file (`packages/pyshared/currency.py`) and nowhere else. Percentages
are unaffected — a 25% saving is 25% in any currency.

## 2. Each part, in plain words

### PR1 — reading the data

**What it does.** Turns six downloaded datasets into clean tables, and marks every
row with where it came from: `real`, `public-synthetic` (someone else's invented
data), or `generated` (invented by us).

**Why.** The first thing a sharp reviewer asks is "is this real?". The only answer
worth giving is one you can prove row by row. Only the SCMS delivery history is
genuinely real. The SAP tables are Google's demo data, not a real SAP export — we
said otherwise in an early draft and corrected it.

**Differently.** Check column types formally at the boundary, not at runtime.

### PR1 — features that cannot cheat

**What it does.** Every fact about a supplier — how often they were late, how much
they shipped, their average delay — uses **only orders that came before** the one
being predicted.

**Why.** Otherwise the model sees the answer. `supplier_prior_late_rate` is the
strongest feature at 0.211 importance; built carelessly it would include the very
order we are predicting, and the model would look brilliant and be useless.

**Differently.** Nothing. This is what we would defend hardest.

### PR1 — three risk models and the gate

**What it does.** Three things we try to predict: late delivery, quality problem,
supply disruption. For each we train three models, compare them against two simple
baselines, and **only ship the model if it clearly wins**. Otherwise we fall back
to the supplier's observed rate and say so on screen.

**Why.** Two of the three lost. Quality has 71 real examples to learn from,
disruption 136. A gate that passes a model trained on 71 examples ships guesswork
— and the optimiser turns these numbers into money, so a bad model changes what we
buy.

**Differently.** With real history all three would probably pass. We would also
give ranges rather than single numbers.

### PR1 — the optimiser (the heart of it)

**What it does.** Decides how much to buy from each supplier, minimising:

```
what we pay  +  (chance of lateness x units x cost of a shortage)
             +  (chance of a defect  x units x cost of rework)
```

subject to: meet demand fully; approved suppliers only; respect each supplier's
capacity; lead time must fit; respect minimum order sizes; at least two suppliers
per material; no supplier over 40% of one material; and honour both halves of each
contract — the ceiling **and the promised minimum**.

**Why.** Risk has to be priced in money, or it is a report sitting next to the
plan. That one choice is why we pay 6.4% more on the invoice and carry 1.3 points
less high-risk volume.

Honouring promised minimums costs real money — 8.1% more on a one-week slice, and
three extra suppliers — because a promise made last year does not care what this
week's cheapest quote is. Where a slice cannot honour every promise at once, we
still return a plan and the message names what was set aside.

**Differently.** A different solver (OR-Tools) if the problem grew ten times
bigger or needed machine changeover scheduling.

### PR1 — comparing against alternatives

**What it does.** Solves the same problem three other ways — cheapest first, split
equally, copy last year — and shows all three.

**Why.** "We saved 25%" means nothing until you say *against what*. We include the
comparison that **beats us** (cheapest-first is cheaper on the invoice), because
that is what makes the rest believable.

**Differently.** Replay the plan week by week over a year instead of one slice.

### PR1 — scenarios

**What it does.** Four disruptions: a supplier stops delivering, demand jumps,
lead times stretch, prices rise. Each changes the inputs and **solves again**.

**Why.** The use case asks for it, and re-solving is what makes it honest. Under an
outage the plan still meets demand in full and costs a few per cent more — which
is the argument for a broad approved supplier base.

**Differently.** Plan against many possible futures at once.

### P2 — the demand forecast

**What it does.** Predicts units per style, 13 weeks ahead, using LightGBM on
recent sales, price, discount and time of year.

**Why.** It beats the standard seasonal baseline by 24% (0.337 against 0.445).
Four of the ten most useful inputs are price and discount — an older statistical
method could not have used any of them.

**Differently.** Predict a range, so safety stock can use the model's own
uncertainty.

### P2 — forecasting a style with no history

**What it does.** For a brand-new style, finds the most similar past style and
scales its curve.

**Why.** Half an apparel range is new every season, so a forecaster that needs
history cannot plan a season. WAPE 0.378 against 0.417 for a category average.

**Say this honestly:** measured on **one** held-out style, 13 weeks. It shows the
method works; it is not a precise accuracy figure.

### P2 — safety stock

**What it does.** Sets buffer stock per style using `z x sigma x root(lead time)`,
then **checks whether the buffer actually worked**.

**Why.** The planned service level is typed in. The achieved one is a result. We
planned 95% and achieved **96.8%** across 2,722 style-weeks.

**Differently.** Size it from the forecast's own uncertainty, and choose the
target by the cost of running out rather than fixing it at 95%.

### P2 — production and fabric

**What it does.** Decides which factory makes what within weekly capacity, then
when to order fabric given minimum order sizes and a five-week lead time.

**Why.** The use case names capacity and fabric lead times as the two things the
sales plan collides with. Peak factory use is exactly 100%, which is *why* there is
a gap to reconcile.

**Differently.** Account for the cost of switching a line between styles, and
re-plan weekly.

### P2 — markdown

**What it does.** Compares how fast each style is really selling against plan, and
for slow ones recommends **which week** to discount and **by how much**.

**Two things to defend.** Once stock is bought its cost is spent, so the question
is revenue against scrap value, not profit margin. Changing that turned
"discounting never pays" into a **₹1.32 crore** recovery.

And the price sensitivity: we tried to measure it and it explained **0% of the
variation**, so we use a published apparel figure of −1.8 and label it *assumed*
on the screen, in the API and in the metrics file.

**Differently.** Measure it on real discount data. First thing we would fix.

### P2 — the S&OP meeting

**What it does.** Puts three disagreeing plans side by side — what sales wants,
what the forecast says, what the factories can make — prices the gap, and records
one agreed number. It can be lowered by hand but **never raised above what the
factories can make**.

**Why.** This is the whole use case. The gap is 102,318 units and **₹47.3 crore**,
and the cap is the discipline the meeting exists to enforce.

**Differently.** Three versions per cycle (good, expected, bad) and an approval
trail showing who agreed what.

### Both — monitoring and the demo pack

**What it does.** Times every request and reports counts, error rate and speed at
`/api/monitoring`. Every model is trained **before** the demo, every random seed is
fixed, and a test fails the build if training code appears in the web layer.

**Why.** "Real-time decisions" and "monitoring" are marked criteria, and both are
easier to claim than to show. This shows them, in about a hundred lines we can all
explain.

## 3. Questions we expect

### About the data

**"Is this real data?"** Partly, and every screen says which part. The 10,324
purchase orders are real. The retail data behind TrendWear is public but **invented
by its author** — labelled everywhere. Supplier capacity, prices and the sales plan
are generated by us. Accuracy is measured on real rows only.

**"Why invent data at all?"** The real dataset has no price quotes, no capacity and
no approved supplier list, and the use case needs all three. Either generate them
and say so, or change the problem. Also: the official brief asks for *synthetic*
data for both use cases, so we are ahead of what was required.

**"Why rupees if the dataset is American?"** Because our audience is Indian. We
convert once, when the data is read, at ₹83 to the dollar, and that rate lives in
one file. We did **not** simply relabel the dollar sign — that would have made
every figure wrong by a factor of 83.

### About the models

**"Your quality model is worse than the baseline. Why is it in the project?"** As
evidence. We trained it, measured it, and it lost. So the application does not use
it — it falls back to the supplier's observed rate and prints "not sufficient" with
the reason. With 71 examples that is the correct outcome.

**"How do you know the models are not cheating?"** Every split is by date — train
on the past, test on the future. And every supplier fact is built only from earlier
orders. The strongest feature is the supplier's past lateness, which is exactly the
one that would have leaked had we been careless.

### About the optimiser

**"What makes this better than buying cheapest?"** It costs 6.4% more on the
invoice and puts 1.3 points less volume with risky suppliers. Priced properly the
total is lower. And cheapest-first cannot obey "at least two suppliers" and the 40%
cap at once — it is not merely worse, it breaks the rules.

**"What if there is no valid answer?"** It relaxes rules in a fixed order — the
concentration cap, then contract ceilings, then promised minimums — and **always
reports what it relaxed**. We hit genuine dead ends four times while building and
fixed each at source.

**"Does it scale?"** Not to a real network, and we will not pretend otherwise.
Hundreds of plants would need a different approach; costed at 8 person-days.

### About P2

**"Why is the agreed number below both the forecast and supply?"** The cap applies
per style per week, not to the total. A style can be short in week 3 and
over-supplied in week 7. Summed, the agreed total sits below both — which is what a
genuinely constrained commitment looks like.

**"Your price sensitivity is assumed. Does that break markdown?"** It limits it.
*When* to discount comes from measured sell-through, which is real. *How much* uses
an assumed figure because the data has no usable price signal, and the screen says
so. Fitting a curve to noise would have been worse.

### About engineering

**"Why no front-end framework?"** Thirteen screens of tables and charts, four
people, one week, and a demo that must work without internet. No build step means
nothing sits between our code and what you see.

**"How would you monitor this in production?"** Today: request counts, error rate
and speed per endpoint. Missing and costed: watching for data drift, alerting when
live accuracy drops below what we published, and a model registry we can roll back.

**"What is the weakest part?"** Supplier capacity. The optimiser is only as good as
those numbers, and today they are inferred from shipping history rather than
confirmed by suppliers.

   sales.
2. **The markdown price sensitivity is assumed**, because our measurement explained
   nothing.
3. **Supplier capacity, prices and the sales plan are generated by us**, with the
   method written down.
4. **Two of our three risk models are not good enough to use**, so we do not use
   them.
