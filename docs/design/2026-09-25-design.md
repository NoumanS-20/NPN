# Design — NPN SCM Hackathon, Team Vortex5 (P2 + PR1)

Date: 2026-09-25
Evaluation: 2026-09-28 (live demo) · Technical interview: 2026-09-30

## 0. Decisions from the mentor and the team (2026-09-25)

- **Two separate applications with two separate demos.** Not one platform with two areas.
- One repository, `NoumanS-20/NPN`, holding `apps/pr1/` (**SupplyGuard**) and `apps/p2/`
  (**TrendWear Planner**) as independent applications with their own backends, frontends, databases and
  deployed URLs.
- **Real Kaggle data mixed with synthetic data is allowed.** Both apps use real history as the base and add
  synthetic records where the real data is too thin.
- PR1 expands to **500+ supplier entities**: 217 real ones from SCMS plus synthetic suppliers calibrated on
  the real price, lead-time, on-time and quality distributions. Real and synthetic are labelled in the data.
- P2 keeps the **20 real clothing styles** as the sales history and adds synthetic styles calibrated on their
  seasonality and elasticity, giving a launch calendar of roughly 60 styles.
- **LLM explanation layer in both apps**, via an open Hugging Face model through hosted inference
  (account `Nouman-20`), with deterministic templates as the fallback.
- Design direction: restrained, high-craft enterprise software — strong typographic hierarchy, generous
  spacing, quiet colour, motion only where it clarifies. Deep blue for SupplyGuard, amber for TrendWear.
- Commits carry no AI attribution. Teammates are collaborators on the repo.
- Slide deck is deferred until the apps are built.

## 1. What we are building

Two **separate, non-overlapping** applications:

| | PR1 — **SupplyGuard** | P2 — **TrendWear S&OP** |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered | Given a material requirement, **which suppliers get what share, and will they deliver?** | **What do we make, when, and when do we discount?** |
| Domain | Health-commodity procurement (real USAID SCMS data) | Apparel retail planning (real retail sales data) |
| Core method | MILP allocation + three risk classifiers | Demand forecasting + lot sizing + markdown optimisation |
| Data | SCMS, SAP, procurement KPI files | Retail store inventory file |

The mentor's written ruling (email, 2026-09-25) requires two different solutions whose approaches do not
overlap. We enforce that as a hard rule:

- **No shared data.** Each product loads its own tables from its own sources.
- **No shared models.** No model is trained once and reused across products.
- **No cross-calls.** PR1 never reads P2 output and P2 never calls PR1's optimiser.
- **Different decisions.** PR1 chooses *between suppliers*. P2 chooses *quantities and timing* from a single
  source of supply. P2's fabric buying is time-phased lot sizing under MOQ and lead time, never allocation.

Shared only: the repository, a small internal library of UI primitives and Python helpers, the test harness,
CI and the deployment workflow. The two applications run as separate processes on separate URLs.

## 2. Datasets

All data is real and downloaded from Kaggle, as the mentor required. Derived fields are documented in
`docs/assumptions.md` and always marked as assumptions in the UI.

### PR1
| File | Rows | What it provides |
|---|---|---|
| `scms/SCMS_Delivery_History_Dataset.csv` | 10,324 POs | **Primary.** 73 vendors × 88 manufacturing sites = **217 supplier entities**, 141 competing in the largest product group; promised vs actual delivery dates (**11.5% late**); unit and pack prices; freight cost; weight; shipment mode; 43 countries; 184 items |
| `sap/lfa1.csv` | 3,035 | **2,590-vendor master** from a real SAP ERP dump — country, city, account group. Used for the enterprise supplier catalogue and the ERP-integration story |
| `sap/ekko,ekpo,eket,ekbe.csv` | 76k / 126k / 168k / 124k | Real SAP purchase orders, items, schedule lines and goods receipts. 158,942 lines with promised vs actual receipt dates (**42% late**) — a second, larger validation set for the delay model |
| `procurement/supplier_order_lines.csv` | 517 | 44 suppliers with **quality events** (13.7%), supplier-fault flags, rework cost, criticality |
| `procurement/procurement_kpi.csv` | 777 POs | **Disruption labels**: 63 cancelled, 73 partially delivered; defective units; negotiated vs list price |
| `procurement/supply_chain_data.csv` | 100 | Lead times, defect rates, inspection results, manufacturing cost |

**Supplier entity** = vendor + manufacturing site, which is how sourcing decisions are actually made.

**How the data maps to the use case.** PR1 describes a manufacturer buying raw materials. SCMS is
health-commodity procurement: a buying organisation sources items from vendors and their manufacturing sites,
against contracts, prices, lead times and delivery promises. The structure is identical — buyer, approved
suppliers, materials, plants, POs, promised and actual deliveries — so we state the mapping openly in the
README, the deck and the demo: *item → material, vendor and manufacturing site → supplier entity, delivery
location → plant.* Nothing is hidden behind a rename.

**Synthetic augmentation.** On top of the 217 real supplier entities we generate synthetic ones, calibrated
on the real distributions of price, lead time, on-time rate, quality and order size, to reach **500+
suppliers**. Every record carries an `origin` field of `real` or `synthetic`, the UI shows it, and all
reported model metrics are computed on real records only. Synthetic suppliers exist to make the allocation
problem large, not to inflate the results.

### P2
| File | Rows | What it provides |
|---|---|---|
| `retail/retail_store_inventory.csv` | 14,626 Clothing rows (of 73,100) | 20 products × 5 stores, daily for 2 years; units sold, inventory, price, **discount 0–20%**, promotion flag, seasonality, weather, competitor pricing |

The discount column gives a **real price-elasticity curve** for markdown decisions instead of an invented one.

**Synthetic augmentation.** The 20 real styles become the calibration set; synthetic styles drawn from their
seasonality, elasticity and volume distributions extend the catalogue to roughly 60 styles laid out on a
6-week launch calendar across three seasons. Forecast accuracy is reported on the real styles; synthetic
styles carry the S&OP cycle, the capacity squeeze and the cold-start demo.

## 3. PR1 — SupplyGuard

### 3.1 Data model (SQLite)
`suppliers` (entity, vendor, site, country, tier) · `materials` · `plants` · `material_requirements`
(material × plant × week) · `approved_supplier_list` (supplier × material × plant, with a qualification date)
· `supplier_offers` (supplier × material: price, lead time, historical volume, min/max order seen) ·
`po_history` (PO with promised/actual dates, quantity, value, freight, mode, outcome labels) · `risk_scores` ·
`allocation_runs` · `allocation_lines` · `scenarios` · `model_metrics`.

**Plants and the requirement plan.** The official data spec asks for a material demand forecast by SKU and
plant. SCMS ships to 43 countries through named delivery locations; we treat the eight highest-volume
locations as **plants** and aggregate historical consumption per material and plant into a forward
**requirement plan** by week. This is a deterministic roll-up of history with a seasonal index — **PR1 does
not train a forecasting model**, because forecasting belongs to P2 and the two approaches must not overlap.
The requirement plan is an input the buyer can also override by hand in the UI.

**Approved supplier list.** Not every supplier may serve every material at every plant. The ASL is built from
observed trading history (a supplier that has delivered that material is approved) plus qualification rules
for synthetic suppliers. The optimiser may only choose from the ASL, which is how real procurement works and
is one of the official data requirements.

### 3.2 Risk models — three targets, as the mentor specified
| Model | Label | Source | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delay | delivered after promised date | SCMS (11.5% positive), validated on SAP (42%) | XGBoost, calibrated probability |
| Quality | material-discrepancy event or defective units | `supplier_order_lines` + `procurement_kpi` | XGBoost |
| Disruption | order cancelled or partially delivered | `procurement_kpi` | Logistic regression (small data, needs to stay interpretable) |

**Model selection is measured, not asserted.** For each target we train logistic regression, random forest
and XGBoost on identical features and the identical time split, publish all three side by side, and keep the
winner. Where the simpler model is within noise of XGBoost we keep the simpler one and say why. We do not use
a complex model to look advanced, and labels are validated before a model is promised: if the quality or
disruption labels do not hold up, we ship two solid models and report the third as insufficient.

Features: supplier's trailing on-time rate and volume, order size versus that supplier's typical order,
lead-time length, shipment mode, country, product group, item value, season, and weight. Strictly
point-in-time: every trailing feature is computed from orders **before** the order being scored, so no leakage.

Metrics: precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC, each against a majority-class and a
"supplier's historical rate" baseline. Reported per dataset, never pooled to flatter the number.
Split by time (train on earlier years, test on later) — not random — because random splits leak the future.

### 3.3 Allocation MILP (PuLP + CBC)

Decide, for each supplier *s*, material *m* and week *w*:
`qty[s,m,w] ≥ 0` and `order[s,m,w] ∈ {0,1}`.

Minimise
`Σ price[s,m]·qty + Σ freight[s,m]·qty + λ_risk·Σ P(late)[s,m]·qty·shortage_cost[m] + λ_quality·Σ P(defect)[s,m]·qty·rework_cost[m]`

subject to
0. **Approved suppliers only:** `qty[s,m,w] = 0` unless `(s, m, plant)` is on the approved supplier list
1. **Demand:** `Σ_s qty[s,m,w] = requirement[m,plant,w]`
2. **Capacity:** `qty[s,m,w] ≤ capacity[s,m]`
3. **MOQ:** `qty[s,m,w] ≥ moq[s,m]·order[s,m,w]` and `qty[s,m,w] ≤ M·order[s,m,w]`
4. **Contract:** `contract_min[s,m] ≤ Σ_w qty[s,m,w] ≤ contract_max[s,m]`
5. **Lead time:** a supplier is eligible for week *w* only if `risk_adjusted_lead_time[s,m] ≤ weeks_available`,
   where the risk adjustment adds the supplier's historical p75 delay
6. **Concentration cap:** no supplier takes more than `max_share` of a material's volume
7. **Single-source guard:** at least `min_suppliers[m]` suppliers per critical material

`λ_risk`, `λ_quality` and `max_share` are UI sliders; the plan re-solves live. Target solve time under
2 seconds for a run of 217 suppliers × up to 12 materials × 8 weeks.

**Baselines we compare against** (this is where the KPI numbers come from): cheapest-supplier-first,
equal split across approved suppliers, and the historical allocation actually seen in the data.

### 3.4 Screens
1. **Requirement intake** — the requirement plan by material, plant and week, editable by hand.
2. **Supplier comparison** — all eligible supplier entities with price, lead time, on-time rate, quality score,
   risk probabilities and a computed total-cost-of-ownership column. Sortable, filterable, hundreds of rows.
3. **Allocation result** — the split with a reason per line ("chosen despite +4% price: 9% lower delay risk"),
   cost breakdown, and the comparison against all three baselines.
4. **Pre-PO risk check** — score a specific draft PO before release, with the top contributing factors.
5. **Scenarios** — supplier outage, +30% demand, +2 weeks lead time, price shock; before/after on cost,
   service level and risk concentration.
6. **Model panel** — metrics, confusion matrices, feature importance, drift over time.

## 4. P2 — TrendWear S&OP

### 4.1 Data model (SQLite)
`styles` · `weekly_sales` · `plants` · `plant_capacity` · `fabric_bom` · `fabric_orders` ·
`dcs` · `stores` · `dc_store_lanes` (transit days, cost per unit, mode) · `merchandising_plan` ·
`consensus_plan` · `inventory_position` · `safety_stock` · `plan_versions` · `plan_lines` ·
`markdown_recommendations` · `forecast_metrics`.

Daily rows are aggregated to weekly per style-store, which is the S&OP planning grain.

### 4.2 Modules
- **Demand forecast.** LightGBM with lag, rolling-mean, price, discount, promotion, seasonality and
  competitor-price features. Baselines: seasonal naive and the dataset's own `Demand Forecast` column, which
  is a genuinely competitive benchmark. Metrics: WAPE, MAPE, bias; time-based split.
- **Cold start.** Every style in the data has history, so we simulate a launch: hold a style out entirely and
  forecast it from nothing. The **primary method is analog similarity** — a weighted average of styles in the
  same category, price band and season, shaped by the observed launch curve. It is explainable line by line
  in the interview and needs no downloads at demo time. As a **benchmark only**, we run **Chronos-Bolt**
  (Amazon, Apache-2.0, from Hugging Face) zero-shot on the same held-out style and publish both scores
  against a category-average baseline. If Chronos wins we say so; if it loses we say that too. The benchmark
  is cut without consequence if time runs short, because the analog method carries the feature.
- **Inventory and safety stock.** Per style-store: safety stock from the forecast error and the replenishment
  lead time (`z · σ_error · √lead_time`, service level configurable, default 95%), a reorder point, and
  current inventory versus target. Feeds the demand-versus-supply gap and flags stock-out and overstock risk.
  The real data's inventory-level column gives the opening position, so the numbers are grounded.
- **Production plan.** LP: weekly production per style across 3 plants, limited by weekly capacity and by
  fabric availability, meeting demand plus the safety-stock target, minimising lost sales plus holding cost.
- **Fabric order plan.** Time-phased lot sizing under MOQ and a 4–6 week lead time, single source per fabric.
  **No supplier choice** — that belongs to PR1.
- **Markdown.** Project end-of-season sell-through; if it falls short of target, recommend the week and depth
  using an elasticity curve fitted on the real discount-versus-units-sold data. Report margin recovered
  against a no-markdown and a fixed-end-of-season-markdown baseline.
- **Reconciliation (the heart of S&OP).** Three numbers exist for every style and week: the **merchandising
  plan** (what the buyers intend to sell, generated as the statistical forecast shifted by each style's
  commercial ambition and season target), the **statistical forecast**, and the **constrained supply plan**
  (what capacity, fabric and lead times actually allow). The reconciliation view shows all three, the gap
  between them in units and in money, and the **consensus plan** agreed at Pre-S&OP, which is the number the
  business commits to. This is what the use case means by "reconcile merchandising forecasts with plant
  capacity and fabric lead times".
- **Distribution.** DC-to-store lanes carry transit days and cost per unit, so a unit produced in week *w*
  reaches a store in week *w + transit*. Store-level availability dates, late-to-store flags and the
  distribution cost per plan version come from this.
- **Rolling S&OP cycle.** The cycle runs month after month across the data, not once. Each month re-plans a
  rolling horizon of the next 13 weeks, carries forward actuals to date, and produces its own set of
  versions. The cockpit shows the last three cycles so the "rolling monthly" requirement is visible rather
  than asserted.
- **Four stages and three functions.** Demand Review, Supply Review, Pre-S&OP and Executive Approval, each
  producing a stored, versioned plan. The UI has a view per function — **Merchandising** (demand, markdown,
  revenue), **Production** (capacity, fabric, shortfalls) and **Logistics** (lanes, transit, store
  availability, distribution cost) — each showing that function's numbers and what it must agree to before
  the stage advances. Cross-functional alignment is then something the panel can see, not a claim.
- **Financial view.** Revenue, gross margin and inventory value per plan version, as the PDF's
  "demand, supply and financial plans" requires.

### 4.3 Screens
S&OP cockpit (the rolling cycle, the four stages, demand vs supply gap, financial roll-up) ·
Reconciliation (merchandising plan vs statistical forecast vs constrained supply, and the consensus number) ·
Demand review — Merchandising view (forecast vs actual, accuracy, cold-start styles, markdown) ·
Supply review — Production view (capacity use, shortfalls, fabric arrivals) ·
Logistics view (DC-to-store lanes, transit, store availability, distribution cost) ·
Inventory & safety stock (cover weeks, reorder points, stock-out and overstock flags) ·
Plan-version comparison.

### 4.4 Mapping to the mentor's P2 pipeline

The mentor's nine-step flow maps onto this design as: dataset → §2 · data processing → ETL · demand
forecasting → forecast · model evaluation → forecast metrics · **inventory and safety stock → §4.2** ·
S&OP planning calculation → production plan, fabric lot sizing, S&OP cycle · P2 result → plan versions and
markdown recommendations · FastAPI → §5 · dashboard → plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, exactly as specified. The App 2 demo follows these nine steps in order.

## 5. Platform

```
apps/
  pr1/                      SupplyGuard — runs standalone on its own port and URL
    backend/  app/ (etl, features, risk, optimizer, scenarios, narrative, api)
    web/      index.html, pages/*.html, js/*.js, css/*.css   (served as static files)
    tests/
    Dockerfile
  p2/                       TrendWear Planner — runs standalone on its own port and URL
    backend/  app/ (etl, forecast, production, fabric, markdown, sop, narrative, api)
    web/      index.html, pages/*.html, js/*.js, css/*.css
    tests/
    Dockerfile
packages/
  pyshared/                 config, database session, logging, metric helpers
  web/                      shared CSS tokens and plain-JS modules (table, chart, api, format)
data/    raw/ (gitignored, re-downloadable) · processed/
docs/    design, assumptions, module guides, interview prep, runbook
scripts/ fetch_data.py
```

Each app is independently runnable, testable and deployable. `packages/` holds only presentation and
plumbing helpers — never data, features, models or planning logic, so the separation rule stays intact.

- **Backend:** Python 3.12, FastAPI, pandas, scikit-learn, LightGBM, XGBoost, PuLP, SQLAlchemy, SQLite.
- **Frontend: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript — no framework and no build step.** ES modules loaded straight
  by the browser, Chart.js from a pinned local copy for charts, and a small hand-written table module
  (sort, filter, paginate) shared by both apps. Chosen so every member can read and defend the front end in
  the technical interview, and because it is literally the "HTML + CSS + JS" the mentor's diagrams specify.
  The table module is ours, about 150 lines, which is easier to explain than a third-party grid.
  Frontend tests run under `node --test` with jsdom, covering the table, formatting and API-client modules.
- **Narrative:** deterministic templates are the shipped behaviour, built in both apps as part of the core
  because "explainable recommendation" is a stated requirement. An open Hugging Face model
  (Qwen2.5-Instruct) through hosted inference is an **optional enhancement, off by default and first on the
  cut list**. Numbers always come from the models, never from the language model, and if inference is
  unavailable the template text is shown and nothing breaks.
- **Tests:** pytest per module — ETL row counts and null rules, no-leakage checks on features, metric floors
  on the models, feasibility and constraint checks on the optimiser (e.g. MOQ respected, demand met,
  concentration cap honoured), and API contract tests. `node --test` with jsdom for the front-end modules.
- **CI/CD:** GitHub Actions runs ruff, pytest and the JavaScript tests for both apps on every push, then
  deploys each app to its own Hugging Face Docker Space under the account `Nouman-20`.
- **Deployment:** one small container per app — Python only, no Node stage, since the frontend is static
  files that FastAPI serves directly. The two Space URLs are the primary demo; laptop runs are the offline
  fallback; recorded video is the last resort.
- **Monitoring:** two layers, both visible in the UI. *Model monitoring* — metrics over time, forecast error
  by week, drift in feature distributions, and an alert when a metric falls below its floor.
  *Application monitoring* — request count, error rate and p50/p95 latency per endpoint, plus a health
  endpoint each Space exposes.
- **Real-time decisions, shown not claimed:** every optimisation and scoring response carries its own
  `solve_seconds`, and the UI prints it next to the result ("re-planned 520 suppliers in 1.4 s"). The same
  numbers feed the latency panel, so the "real-time decisions" criterion has evidence behind it.
- **Reusability:** the risk models, the optimiser and the planning modules each take plain dataframes and a
  config object, never a database handle, so they can be lifted into another project. Data sources are
  declared in one config file per app; pointing either app at a different dataset means editing that file and
  the ETL module, nothing else. `docs/reusability.md` states what is reusable, what is specific, and what an
  adopter would have to change.

### 5.1 Demo reliability rules

The evaluation is a live demo in a room, so the build treats the demo as a deliverable in its own right.

- **Nothing is trained during the demo.** Models are trained ahead of time, validated, and saved as artifacts
  with their metrics. The demo runs inference and optimisation only.
- **Everything is reproducible.** Random seeds, data versions and model versions are pinned, so the same
  scenario produces the same numbers every time it is run.
- **Two scenarios are preloaded** in each app — one clean baseline and one disruption — and the panel can
  change inputs (weights, quantities, an outage) and re-run without any retraining.
- **Solve time is shown** beside each result, which is also the evidence for "real-time decisions".
- **An offline build is tested** on the demo laptop the day before, and a recorded video is the last resort.
- **A pre-demo checklist** in `docs/runbook.md` is run twice: on 27 September and on the morning of the 28th.

### 5.2 Enterprise integration story

The prototype reads exported files; it does not connect to a live ERP, and we say so plainly. What we show is
where a production system would attach:

```
ERP / SAP  →  scheduled extract or API  →  validation and transformation  →  planning engine (ours)
           →  planner review and approval  →  allocation or plan output  →  back to ERP for execution
```

Our FastAPI layer is that attachment point: every calculation is already an API, so the change from prototype
to production is the data source and an outbound write, not a rewrite. `docs/architecture.md` carries both
diagrams — what we built, and what a production deployment would look like.

## 6. Assumptions we add to real data

Every one is listed in `docs/assumptions.md` with its derivation, and flagged in the UI.

| Field | How it is derived |
|---|---|
| PR1 plants | the eight highest-volume SCMS delivery locations |
| PR1 requirement plan | historical consumption per material and plant, rolled forward with a seasonal index |
| PR1 approved supplier list | suppliers with observed history for that material, plus qualified synthetic suppliers |
| P2 merchandising plan | statistical forecast shifted by a per-style commercial ambition factor and the season's sales target |
| P2 DC-to-store lanes | one DC per region, transit days and cost scaled by distance band |
| Supplier capacity | p95 of that supplier's historical quantity per period, scaled |
| MOQ | p10 of historical order quantity for that supplier-material |
| Contract min/max | share bands around the supplier's historical share of the material |
| Shortage and rework cost | multiples of item value, stated per material |
| P2 fabric BOM | metres per unit per clothing style, fixed table |
| P2 plant capacity | derived from observed peak weekly sales across the network |

## 7. Non-goals

No chatbot (neither use case asks for one). No procure-to-pay, OCR or invoice matching (that is PR2). No
real ERP connection — we read exported SAP tables. No authentication or multi-tenancy. No mobile app.
